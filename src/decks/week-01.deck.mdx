## Three words that are not synonyms

- A **hypha** is one fungal filament
- A **mycelium** is those filaments as a body
- A **mycorrhiza** is the structure hyphae and a root build together — neither partner makes it alone

<p class="caption"><span class="ai-tag">AI generated</span> Teaching diagram, not a micrograph.</p>

</div>
</div>

## Two associations, one word

Most of what this course will call "the network" is one of two structures. They are not interchangeable.

| Feature | Arbuscular (AMF) | Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interface | Arbuscule *inside* the cortical cell wall | Hartig net *between* cells, plus a sheath |
| Fungi | Glomeromycota | Mostly Basidiomycota and Ascomycota |
| Typical hosts | Most vascular plant species | A minority of species, many of them forest trees |

Both get called mycorrhizal networks. Neither is automatically a network.

## AMF: the fungus goes in

Hyphae enter the root cortex. Inside a cortical cell they branch into an **arbuscule** — the tree-shaped interface where phosphate and carbon are exchanged.

They do not enter the cytoplasm. The plant membrane wraps the fungus.

## ECM: the fungus stays out

Three parts, when the association is typical:

1. A **sheath** (mantle) around the root
2. A **Hartig net** between cortical cells — the exchange surface
3. Extraradical mycelium into the soil

The cell wall is not crossed.

## What that looks like on a pine

*Lactarius deliciosus* on *Pinus*: swollen, forked tips; a mantle in cross-section; hyphae of the Hartig net running *between* cortical cells.

Short, fat, often dichotomous tips are a field mark. They are still one plant and its fungus.

## Who actually hosts whom

AMF are common as species; ECM are common as temperate forest biomass.

- About **72%** of vascular plant species form arbuscular associations; about **2%** form ectomycorrhizal ones (Brundrett & Tedersoo, 2018)
- Ectomycorrhizal trees are estimated at about **60% of stems** globally, and more than **80%** outside the tropics (Steidinger et al., 2019)

A rare association can still dominate a biome. That is not a network claim. It is a census.

## Proximity is not a credential

Two plants in the same soil, even with the same fungal *species*, can be unconnected. A **network** here needs a shared fungal **individual** — a genet — and measured movement of carbon or nutrients between named hosts through it.

<p class="caption"><span class="ai-tag">AI generated</span> Schema of the distinction, not a map of any forest.</p>

</div>

## What "connection" has to mean

Not co-occurrence. Not a fruiting body near a trunk. Not a documentary voiceover.

Before this course licenses the word *network*:

1. One fungal individual, spanning more than one host
2. Carbon or nutrient flow between those hosts, through that individual, actually measured
3. Alternative paths (soil diffusion, overlapping roots) not left as the equally good explanation

Beiler et al. (2010) mapped *Rhizopogon* genets linking Douglas-fir cohorts. That is architecture. Architecture is not transfer.

<p class="kicker">Where the metaphor already strains</p>

## "Credentials" overclaims too

A credential is issued. Someone recognises someone.

The soil does not. A mycorrhiza can exist without a bureau, and a common mycelium can exist without anyone being "admitted" to anything.

Keep the test. Notice the costume. Week 1 is not exempt from the rule it writes.

---

## Three popular claims, one review

Karst, Jones & Hoeksema (2023, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*) took the headlines to the literature.

- That common mycorrhizal networks are widespread in forests: **not settled**. Field results vary, and other explanations remain open
- That resources moving through those networks improve seedling performance: **not shown well enough to generalise**
- That mature trees preferentially send resources and defence signals to their offspring: **no peer-reviewed evidence**

This is the kind of sentence the credentials test is for. Week 3 will sit with the carbon-transfer papers themselves.
